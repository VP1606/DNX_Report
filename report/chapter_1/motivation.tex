\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_motivation}

Legislation is pushing automotive manufacturers to adopt increasingly complex autonomous driving systems, on a trajectory that will ultimately require full, real-time spatial understanding of the surrounding environment. 
Beyond this scaling pressure, the dense \acrfullpl{ann} currently powering automotive vision suffer a paradigm-specific weakness: their static, frame-based nature results in billions of dense \gls{mac} operations per frame regardless of scene content, generating substantial thermal, latency and power pressure even on visually redundant scenes.

To resolve these constraints, this research looks toward the most power-efficient perception engine known: the biological brain. 
Unlike frame-based cameras, biological retinas and neuromorphic event sensors transmit only asynchronous luminance changes, allowing biological neural networks to consume metabolic energy only when processing new information \cite{pfeiffer_snn_opportunities}.

\acrfullpl{snn} are built around this same principle of activation sparsity. 
By replacing continuous \gls{mac} operations with discrete, event-driven \acrfull{ac} additions, they offer a theoretical pathway to sub-milliwatt, real-time object detection at the edge. 
Since spiking neurons remain dormant until triggered, they naturally sidestep the thermal and power pressure that dense \gls{cnn} inference places on resource-constrained automotive hardware \cite{lin_adas_cnn_issues}.

However, the transition from 32-bit floating-point activations to 1-bit discrete spikes introduces quantisation errors and temporal precision limits. 
Historically, this has restricted \glspl{snn} to low-resolution classification tasks, leaving a significant gap in their deployment for high-definition automotive perception.

The \texttt{DetectorNX} project is motivated by the need to measure and mitigate these trade-offs on modern, high-definition event datasets. 
By establishing strict architectural parity between an \gls{snn} and a \gls{cnn} baseline, this project determines whether the neuromorphic paradigm's thermal and power savings can be achieved without fatally compromising the spatial precision required for autonomous vehicle safety.
